### TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)

- **Units**: mIU/L (= mU/L = µIU/mL). Conversion factor 1 in all directions; there is no separate SI unit.
- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - LabCorp (test 004259, "Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH)"): ">10 y: 0.450−4.500 μIU/mL"; method "Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA)" on the Roche cobas. No sex-specific range. https://www.labcorp.com/tests/004259/thyroid-stimulating-hormone-tsh [S1]
  - ARUP (test 0070145): "20 years and older 0.27-4.20 mU/L"; "12 - 19 years 0.53-3.59"; ECLIA. No sex-specific range. https://ltd.aruplab.com/Tests/Pub/0070145 [S2]
  - Neither lab publishes a sex-specific adult TSH range; both ranges are the manufacturer's (Roche) age-banded intervals.
- **Where that range came from**: The 0.4/0.45 to 4.1/4.5 mIU/L family of ranges rests on NHANES III (1988–1994). Hollowell et al. 2002 measured TSH in 17,353 US residents aged 12 and over; 16,533 were "disease-free" (no reported thyroid disease, goitre or thyroid medication) and 13,344 formed a "reference population" after additionally excluding pregnancy, androgen/oestrogen use, positive thyroid antibodies and biochemical thyroid dysfunction [S3]. The abstract gives only a geometric mean (1.40 mIU/L) and says TSH "was higher in females than males" and that "positive antibodies were more prevalent in women than men" (TgAb 10.4%, TPOAb 11.3% overall) [S3]. The 2.5th–97.5th percentile limits of that reference population are stated in the AACE/ATA 2012 hypothyroidism guideline, which attributes them to NHANES III: the disease-free population has "the upper normal of serum TSH levels is 4.5 mIU/L", while the reference population "had an upper normal TSH value of 4.12 mIU/L", and "the range used should be based on the NHANES III reference population range of 0.45-4.12" [S4]. Surks, Goswami and Daniels 2005 describe the same group as "the Reference Group of NHANES III, 14,333 people more than 12 yr old" and report that "85% had TSH levels below 2.5 mIU/liter" [S5]. So "0.4–4.5" is the disease-free population and "0.45–4.12" is the antibody-negative reference population; neither was derived separately for women.
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: There is no sex-specific adult TSH range in any guideline or lab page opened. The only female-specific thresholds are pregnancy thresholds. ATA 2017 (Alexander et al.), Recommendation 26: "When available, population and trimester-specific reference ranges for serum TSH during pregnancy should be defined by a provider's institute / laboratory... Reference ranges should be defined in healthy, TPOAb-negative pregnant women with optimal iodine intake and without thyroid illness. (Strong recommendation, High quality evidence)"; and "If internal or transferable pregnancy-specific TSH reference ranges are not available, an upper reference limit of ~ 4.0mU/l may be used. For most assays, this represents a reduction in the non-pregnant TSH upper reference limit of ~0.5 mU/L. (Strong recommendation, Moderate quality evidence)" [S6]. The narrative text adds: "In the first trimester, the lower reference range of TSH can be reduced by approximately 0.4 mU/L, while the upper reference range is reduced by approximately 0.5 mU/L... This reference limit should generally be applied beginning with the late first trimester, weeks 7-12, with a gradual return towards the non-pregnant range in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters" [S6]. The ATA public summary adds a preconception target for women already on levothyroxine: "a TSH between the lower end of the reference range and no more than 2.5 mU/L prior to conception" [S7].
  - Age-specific: Surks and Hollowell 2007 (NHANES III disease-free population) report "The 97.5 centiles for the 20-29 and 80+ groups were 3.56 and 7.49 mIU/liter, respectively" and conclude "The prevalence of SCH may be significantly overestimated unless an age-specific range for TSH is used" [S8]. The 2012 AACE/ATA guideline repeats that people over 80 without antibodies have "a 23.9% prevalence of TSH values between 2.5 and 4.5 mIU/L, and a 12% prevalence of TSH concentrations above 4.5 mIU/L" [S4].
- **What is contested**:
  - The upper limit. The AACE/ATA 2012 guideline: "The normal range for TSH values, with an upper limit of 4.12 mIU/L is largely based on NHANES III (11) data, but it has not been universally accepted. Some have proposed that the upper normal should be either 2.5 or 3.0 mIU/L (86)" because "The distribution of TSH values used to establish the normal reference range is skewed to the right by values between 3.1 and 4.12 mIU/L" and "The mean and median values of approximately 1.5 mIU/L are much closer to the lower limit"; the counter-argument is that lowering to 2.5 "would lead to more than 10 million additional diagnoses of hypothyroidism in the United States per year—without clear-cut benefit" [S4]. Wartofsky and Dickey 2005 argued for narrowing: "Recent laboratory guidelines from the National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry indicate that more than 95% of normal individuals have TSH levels below 2.5 mU/liter" [S9]; Surks et al. 2005 replied that "Many with URR TSH do not have thyroid disease" and "About half of those with URR TSH probably have thyroid disease" [S5].
  - The AACE "0.3 to 3.0" proposal. A 2019-dated secondary article (The Hospitalist, Diana Mahoney) states there is "strong clinical rationale for adopting the even narrower TSH reference range of 0.3–3.0 mU/L recommended by the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists in 2003" [S10]. The original AACE press release (January 2003, Thyroid Awareness Month) could not be opened (archive.org is blocked for this tool), and the AACE 2002 guideline abstract (Baskin et al., Endocr Pract 2002;8:457-469, DOI 10.4158/1934-2403-8.6.457) does not state a numeric range [S11]. **The 0.3–3.0 figure is therefore verified only from secondary sources.**
  - Sex and age. The reference population was mixed-sex; the primary paper reports TSH higher in women and antibody positivity more common in women but publishes no sex-specific limits [S3]. Autoimmune thyroid disease is "5-10 times more common in women than in men" [S4], so the antibody-exclusion step removes proportionally more women. The age shift (3.56 at 20–29 versus 7.49 at 80+) means a single adult range over-diagnoses older people and, if anything, is loose for young adults [S8].
  - Treatment of the band. The BMJ Rapid Recommendation (Bekkering et al. 2019; 21 trials, 2,192 participants) issues a strong recommendation against thyroid hormones for adults with subclinical hypothyroidism; "It does not apply to women who are trying to become pregnant or patients with TSH >20 mIU/L" and may not apply to patients with severe symptoms or young adults [S12].
- **What a reader with a value inside the contested band (roughly 2.5 to 4.5 mIU/L, or 4.5 to 10 with normal free T4) is told**:
  - AACE/ATA 2012, Recommendation 16: "Treatment based on individual factors for patients with TSH levels between the upper limit of a given laboratory's reference range and 10 mIU/L should be considered particularly if patients have symptoms suggestive of hypothyroidism, positive TPOAb or evidence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or associated risk factors for these diseases. Grade B, BEL 1" [S4]. On 2.5–4.5: "there are virtually no clinical outcome data to support treating patients with subclinical hypothyroidism with TSH levels between 2.5 and 4.5 mIU/L" [S4].
  - ETA 2013 (Pearce et al.): SCH is divided into "mildly increased TSH levels (4.0-10.0 mU/l)" and >10 mU/l; "replacement therapy with L-thyroxine is recommended for younger patients (<65-70 years) with serum TSH >10 mU/l"; younger patients with TSH <10 and symptoms may be given a trial, stopped if no improvement; the oldest old with TSH ≤10 are managed by watchful waiting [S13].
  - BMJ 2019: strong recommendation against thyroid hormones for adults with SCH, with the exclusions quoted above [S12].
  - Pregnancy, ATA 2017 Recommendation 29: "Levothyroxine therapy is recommended for: - TPO antibody positive women with a TSH greater than the pregnancy specific reference range... - TPO antibody negative women with a TSH greater than 10.0 mU/L." "Levothyroxine therapy may be considered for: - TPO antibody positive women with TSH concentrations > 2.5 mU/L and below the upper limit of the pregnancy specific reference range. (Weak recommendation, Moderate quality evidence) - TPO antibody negative women with TSH concentrations greater than the pregnancy specific reference range and below 10.0 mU/L. (Weak recommendation, Low quality evidence)". "Levothyroxine therapy is not recommended for: - TPO antibody negative women with a normal TSH (TSH within the pregnancy specific reference range, or < 4.0 mU/L if unavailable)." Recommendation 28: "Pregnant women with TSH concentrations >2.5 mU/L should be evaluated for TPO antibody status" [S6].
- **Confidence**: high for the lab ranges, NHANES III design, age centiles, ATA 2017 and BMJ 2019 wording; medium for the 0.45–4.12 limits (read in the 2012 guideline's account of NHANES III rather than Hollowell's own tables); low for the AACE 2003 "0.3–3.0" figure (secondary only).

### Free T4 and free T3 (brief)

- **Units**: free T4 ng/dL; SI pmol/L; 1 ng/dL = 12.87 pmol/L (standard factor; not separately verified this session). Free T3 pg/mL; SI pmol/L; 1 pg/mL = 1.536 pmol/L (standard factor; not separately verified).
- **Typical lab reference range**: LabCorp free T4 (test 001974, "Thyroxine (T4) Free, Direct"): ">19 y: 0.82−1.77 ng/dL", ECLIA on Roche cobas, no sex-specific values; the table cites a Roche Diagnostics source [S1]. LabCorp free T3 (test 010389): ">19 y: 2.0−4.4 pg/mL", ECLIA, no sex-specific values; the footnote cites "Reference Intervals for Children and Adults, Elecsys Thyroid Test, Roche Diagnostics, May 2005" [S2].
- **Where that range came from**: The manufacturer's (Roche Elecsys) reference-interval study, as cited by the lab; the underlying population is not described on the lab pages. Unverified beyond that.
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: None found for non-pregnant adults. Pregnancy: ATA 2017 Recommendation 2: "The accuracy of serum Free T4 measurement by the indirect analog immunoassays is influenced by pregnancy and also varies significantly by manufacturer. If measured in pregnant women, assay method-specific and trimester-specific pregnancy reference ranges should be applied. (Strong recommendation, Moderate quality evidence)" [S3]. Recommendation 3 prefers total T4 "with a pregnancy-adjusted reference range" in later pregnancy [S3].
- **What is contested**: Free T4 immunoassays are indirect analog methods whose results are "influenced by pregnancy" and vary "significantly by manufacturer" [S3]; the guideline notes equilibrium dialysis with LC-MS/MS as the reference method but "not widely available due to high instrument and operating costs" [S3]. Isolated hypothyroxinaemia in pregnancy is defined relative to "the lower 2.5th -5th percentile of a given population" [S3].
- **What a reader in the band is told**: ATA 2017 Recommendation 30: "Isolated hypothyroxinemia should not be routinely treated in pregnancy. (Weak recommendation, Low quality evidence)" [S3].
- **Confidence**: medium. Lab ranges verified; population basis is a manufacturer insert not opened.
- **Sources**:

  2. [S2] LabCorp test 010389 Triiodothyronine (T3), Free. https://www.labcorp.com/tests/010389/triiodothyronine-t-free
  3. [S3] Alexander EK et al. ATA 2017 pregnancy guideline. Thyroid 2017;27:315-389. DOI 10.1089/thy.2016.0457

### Vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D)

- **Units**: ng/mL (conventional) and nmol/L (SI); 1 ng/mL = 2.5 nmol/L as used in the guidelines (20 ng/mL = 50 nmol/L; 30 ng/mL = 75 nmol/L) [S2, S3]; the exact factor is 2.496.
- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - ARUP (test 2002348, 25-Hydroxyvitamin D2 and D3 by Tandem Mass Spectrometry), ages 18 and older: "Deficiency: less than 20 ng/mL; Insufficiency: 20-29 ng/mL; Optimum level: 30-80 ng/mL; Possible toxicity: greater than 150 ng/mL"; HPLC-MS/MS. For ages 1–17: deficiency <20, optimum ≥20. No sex-specific values [S1].
  - LabCorp (test 081950, Vitamin D, 25-Hydroxy): the reference-range cell lists "20.0−100.0 ng/mL", "30−100 ng/mL" and "20.0−100.0 ng/mL" without labels; the body text says deficiency is below 20 ng/mL citing the IOM and Endocrine Society, and that the Endocrine Society defines insufficiency as 21–29 ng/mL; ICMA on DiaSorin LIAISON; no sex-specific values [S5]. The unlabeled table is itself an example of the 20-versus-30 ambiguity.
- **Where that range came from**: Two 2011 documents. (a) The Institute of Medicine DRI report: "A level of 40 nmol/L (16 ng/mL) was consistent with the intended nature of an average requirement" (the EAR), EARs and RDAs "are specified on the basis of serum 25OHD concentrations of 40 and 50 nmol/L (16 and 20 ng/mL), respectively", and "Practically all persons are sufficient at serum 25OHD levels of at least 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL)" [S2]. The clinicians' summary (Ross et al. 2011) states the RDA corresponds "to a serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level of at least 20 ng/ml (50 nmol/liter)" and "The Committee concluded that the prevalence of vitamin D inadequacy in North America has been overestimated" [S2b]. (b) The Endocrine Society guideline (Holick et al. 2011), Recommendation 1.2: "Vitamin D deficiency is defined as a 25(OH)D below 20 ng/ml (50 nmol/liter)" and "vitamin D insufficiency as a 25(OH)D of 21–29 ng/ml"; the evidence text suggests "sufficiency as a 25(OH)D of 30–100 ng/ml" [S3]. The ARUP categories (<20, 20–29, 30–80) reproduce the Endocrine Society 2011 scheme. The 30 ng/mL figure was, per the same guideline, a different reading of the same bone histomorphometry data that the IOM read as supporting 20 ng/mL [S3].
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: None. The IOM report sets the same vitamin D EAR/RDA for men and women ("After the age of 70 years, the effects of aging on bone loss resulted in EARs and RDAs that are the same for men and women"; sex-specific values at 51–70 apply to calcium, not vitamin D) [S2]. The Endocrine Society 2024 guideline suggests empiric supplementation in pregnancy (Recommendation 8: "We suggest empiric vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy") but no pregnancy-specific 25(OH)D threshold [S4]. UK SACN 2016 uses a single population threshold for everyone: "Evidence suggests that the risk of poor musculoskeletal health is increased at serum 25(OH)D concentrations below 25 nmol/L. This concentration therefore represents a 'population protective level'" and "a serum concentration of 25 nmol/L is not a clinical threshold diagnostic of the disease" [S7].
- **What is contested**:
  - 20 versus 30 ng/mL. IOM committee members responded in 2012 that they "respond to aspects of The Endocrine Society guideline that are not well supported" (Rosen et al. 2012) [S6]. In 2024 the Endocrine Society withdrew its own target: the panel "no longer endorses the target 25(OH)D level of 30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L)" and no longer endorses "specific 25(OH)D levels to define vitamin D sufficiency"; it notes "it is possible that there is no single threshold 25(OH)D level appropriate for the entire general population" and "appropriate thresholds likely vary based on the outcome of interest" [S4].
  - Population target misread as an individual cut-off. The IOM's 20 ng/mL is the level at which "Practically all persons are sufficient" (an RDA-type value covering 97.5% of people), with the average requirement at 16 ng/mL [S2]. Manson, Brannon, Rosen and Taylor (NEJM 2016, DOI 10.1056/NEJMp1608005) argue this point in "Vitamin D Deficiency — Is There Really a Pandemic?"; the article is paywalled and its text was **not opened**, so its wording is unverified here [S8].
  - Screening. USPSTF 2021: "The USPSTF found that the evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of screening for vitamin D deficiency" (I statement), and "no one serum vitamin D level cutpoint defines deficiency, and no consensus exists regarding the precise serum levels that represent optimal health or sufficiency" [S9]. Endocrine Society 2024 Recommendation 12: "In healthy adults, we suggest against routine screening for 25(OH)D levels", with the same advice for adults under 50, 50–74, 75 and older, pregnant individuals, and adults with dark complexion or obesity [S4].
  - No sex-specific evidence base: subgroup findings in the 2024 systematic reviews "were not modified by risk of bias, sex, dosage of vitamin D, or calcium co-administration" (kidney outcomes, 50–74) and for fracture in adults 75+ "Study subgroup analyses did not implicate sex" [S4].
- **What a reader with a value in the contested band (20–29 ng/mL, labelled "insufficient" by ARUP and by Endocrine Society 2011) is told**: Endocrine Society 2011 defines this as "vitamin D insufficiency" [S3]; IOM 2011 says that at 20 ng/mL "Practically all persons are sufficient" [S2]; Endocrine Society 2024 "no longer endorses" the 30 ng/mL target and suggests "against empiric vitamin D supplementation above the current DRI" in healthy adults under 75, while suggesting empiric supplementation for those "aged 75 years and older" and "during pregnancy" [S4]; USPSTF: insufficient evidence to screen at all [S9].
- **Confidence**: high. All three guidelines, the IOM report text, USPSTF and SACN were opened directly; the lab pages were opened. Only the NEJM 2016 perspective is unverified.

### Testosterone in women (total testosterone)

- **Units**: ng/dL (conventional) and nmol/L (SI); 1 ng/dL = 0.0347 nmol/L (34.7 pmol/L), consistent with Braunstein 2011's 15–46 ng/dL = 520–1595 pmol/L [S4].
- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - ARUP (test 0081058, "Testosterone (Adult Females, Children, or Individuals on Testosterone-Suppressing Hormone Therapy)", HPLC-MS/MS, ng/dL): female "18-39 years 9-55", "40-59 years 9-55", "60 years and older 5-32", "Premenopausal (18 years and older) 9-55", "Postmenopausal 5-32"; male 18-39 years 300-1080 [S1]. Sex-specific: yes, and ARUP's immunoassay test (0070130) states that no intervals are established for cisgender females on that method [S1b].
  - LabCorp (test 004226, Testosterone, Total, ECLIA on Roche cobas, ng/dL): female "20 to 30 y 13–71", "31 to 40 y 8–60", "41 to 60 y 4–50", "61 to 80 y 3–67"; male 20–80+ y 264–916; "Adult male reference interval is based on a population of healthy nonobese males (BMI <30) between 19 and 39 years old" (no equivalent statement for the female interval) [S2].
- **Where that range came from**: The female LC-MS/MS ranges in the literature come from small or single-cohort studies. Haring et al. 2012: 985 women aged 20–80 from the Study of Health in Pomerania (1,357 including oral-contraceptive and hormone-therapy users), LC-MS/MS, quantile regression; overall reference range (2.5th–97.5th percentile) for total testosterone 0.35–1.97 nmol/L (about 10–57 ng/dL), declining with age; the abstract's premise is that immunoassay measurements "are imprecise and nonspecific at the low concentrations seen in women" [S3]. Braunstein et al. 2011: 161 healthy normally cycling women aged 18–49, morning samples in three cycle phases, "validated immunoassays"; 5th–95th percentile total testosterone for a typical 30-year-old 15–46 ng/dL; the mid-cycle rise was small relative to variability so the ranges can be applied regardless of cycle day [S4]. The lab pages do not state which population underlies their female intervals.
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: The reference intervals are sex-specific by construction (female 9–55 versus male 300–1080 ng/dL at ARUP) [S1]. There is no society-endorsed diagnostic threshold for low testosterone in women. Endocrine Society 2014 (Wierman et al.), Recommendation 1.1: "We recommend against making a clinical diagnosis of androgen deficiency syndrome in healthy women" "because there is a lack of a well-defined syndrome" and "data correlating androgen levels with specific signs or symptoms are unavailable"; the evidence text cites "the lack of valid reference ranges for women" and "the lack of standardized, accurate assays for androgens at the low levels found in women" [S5]. Global Consensus Position Statement 2019 (Davis et al.), Recommendation 3c: "No cutoff blood level can be used for any measured circulating androgen to differentiate women with and without sexual dysfunction"; key message: "A blood total testosterone level should not be used to diagnose HSDD"; Recommendation 12: "Dosing should be limited to achieving testosterone concentrations in the physiologic premenopausal range" [S6].
- **What is contested**:
  - Assay accuracy. Davis et al. 2019, Recommendation 1: "Total testosterone can be measured with high accuracy and reproducibility using liquid/gas chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry assays" (Grade B) and "Direct assays for the measurement of total and free testosterone are highly unreliable in the female range" (Grade A) [S6]. The Endocrine Society position statement on testosterone assays (Rosner et al. 2007) concluded that proficiency testing "should be based on the ability to measure accurately and precisely" known concentrations and that "normative values for total and free testosterone should be established for both genders and children" [S7]; its full text (with the specific statements about women and children at low concentrations) is paywalled and was **not opened**. LabCorp's widely used female range is produced on an ECLIA immunoassay [S2], the class of method the 2019 consensus calls "highly unreliable in the female range" [S6]; ARUP, by contrast, declines to give a female interval for its immunoassay and routes women to LC-MS/MS [S1, S1b].
  - Reference-range basis. The two best-known female range studies rest on 985 women (one German region) [S3] and 161 women (immunoassay) [S4]; Wierman et al. note "the lack of valid reference ranges for women" and that mass spectrometry methods "do not necessarily yield identical answers" [S5].
  - No "low" threshold. Both 2014 and 2019 statements say a blood level cannot define deficiency or sexual dysfunction in women [S5, S6], yet labs flag values below the lower limit (9 ng/dL at ARUP, 13 ng/dL at LabCorp for 20–30 y) as abnormal [S1, S2].
- **What a reader with a value in the band is told**: "We recommend against making a clinical diagnosis of androgen deficiency syndrome in healthy women" [S5]; "A blood total testosterone level should not be used to diagnose HSDD" and "No cutoff blood level can be used for any measured circulating androgen to differentiate women with and without sexual dysfunction" [S6]. Wierman et al. 2014 also report that "Endogenous T levels did not predict response to therapy" in postmenopausal women with HSDD [S5].
- **Confidence**: high for the lab ranges and both consensus statements (opened directly); medium for the population basis (abstracts only for Haring and Braunstein; Rosner full text not opened).

### AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone)

- **Units**: ng/mL (conventional) and pmol/L (SI); 1 ng/mL = 7.14 pmol/L (standard factor; not separately verified this session). Results are assay-dependent (see below).
- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - LabCorp (test 500183, AMH, electrochemiluminescence, ng/mL), female: "20 to 25 y 1.23 to 11.51 (median 4.70)", "26 to 30 y 1.03 to 11.10 (4.20)", "31 to 35 y 0.66 to 8.75 (3.00)", "36 to 40 y 0.42 to 8.34 (1.69)", "41 to 46 y 0.26 to 5.81 (0.58)", "47 to 54 y ≤0.82 (<0.03)", "55 to 97 y ≤0.18"; male 20–98 y 0.11–13.07 [S1]. Sex- and age-specific: yes.
  - ARUP (test 2002656, ELISA, laboratory-developed test): female "18–29 years 0.401–16.015", "30–39 years 0.176–11.705", "40–45 years 6.282 or less", "46–50 years 0.064 or less", "Post-menopausal 0.003 or less" ng/mL [S2].
  - The two labs' lower limits for a woman aged 30–35 differ by a factor of about 4 (0.66 versus 0.176 ng/mL).
- **Where that range came from**: Lab pages give age-banded percentiles without naming the population. The ranges are method-specific because no international standard existed until recently: Ferguson et al. 2020 (NIBSC/WHO) tested 21 method-platform combinations across seven laboratories, of which 16 met validity criteria; the candidate standard 16/190 was "within the limits of acceptable commutability for 6 methods", "partially commutable for a further 3 methods and non-commutable when measured by 7 methods"; the WHO Expert Committee agreed "to establish 16/190 as a WHO Reference Reagent for AMH" (a reference reagent, not yet an International Standard), content 489 ng/ampoule [S3].
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: AMH ranges are female- and age-specific by nature. No guideline sets a fertility threshold. ACOG Committee Opinion 773 (2019): a single AMH measurement "should not be used for counseling patients in this regard" (time to pregnancy) in women not diagnosed with infertility, and routine AMH testing to predict pregnancy loss "is not recommended" [S4]. ASRM 2020: ovarian-reserve markers "are weak predictors of reproductive potential when age is taken into account" [S5].
- **What is contested**: Steiner et al. 2017 (JAMA; prospective cohort, 750 women aged 30–44 analysed of 981 enrolled, no infertility history): low AMH (<0.7 ng/mL, n = 84) versus normal (0.7–8.4 ng/mL): cumulative probability of conception by 6 cycles 65% (95% CI 50%–75%) versus 62% (57%–66%), by 12 cycles 84% (70%–91%) versus 75% (70%–79%); hazard ratio unadjusted 0.96 (0.72–1.28), adjusted 1.19 (0.88–1.61). Conclusion: biomarkers of diminished ovarian reserve "were not associated with reduced fertility", and the findings do not support using AMH to assess natural fertility in such women [S6]. Meanwhile the ranges above are printed with "low" flags against age bands, and direct-to-consumer AMH tests market the number as a fertility measure; ACOG's committee opinion exists precisely to address women "not seeking fertility care" [S4]. Assay non-commutability (7 of 16 methods non-commutable with the WHO reagent) means a value below one lab's lower limit may be inside another's [S3].
- **What a reader with a low-for-age value is told**: ACOG 773: AMH "should not be used for counseling patients in this regard" (time to pregnancy) when they have not been diagnosed with infertility [S4]. ASRM 2020: markers "are weak predictors of reproductive potential when age is taken into account" [S5]. Steiner et al.: findings "do not support" using AMH "to assess natural fertility" [S6].
- **Confidence**: high for Steiner, lab ranges and the WHO reagent paper; medium for ACOG/ASRM (abstracts only, full texts paywalled or 403).

### Prolactin (brief)

- **Units**: ng/mL (= µg/L) conventional; mIU/L SI. Endocrine Society 2011: "1 μg/liter equals 21.2 mIU/liter when the WHO Standard 84/500 is used" [S3].
- **Typical lab reference range**: ARUP (test 0070115, chemiluminescent immunoassay): "10 years and older: Male 2.1-17.7 ng/mL; Female 2.8-29.2 ng/mL"; interpretive ranges "pregnant 9.7->200.0 ng/mL" and "postmenopausal 1.8-20.3 ng/mL" [S1]. LabCorp (test 004465, ECLIA, Roche package insert): female 13–50 y "4.8−33.4", male 13–30 y "3.6−31.5", male 31–50 y "3.9−22.7" ng/mL [S2]. Both labs give sex-specific ranges.
- **Where that range came from**: Manufacturer reference-interval studies (LabCorp cites the Roche package insert) [S2]; population not described on the lab pages. Endocrine Society 2011: "Assay-specific normal values are higher in women than in men and are generally lower than 25 μg/liter" [S3].
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: The labs' female/male intervals above [S1, S2]; the guideline states the sex difference qualitatively without a numeric cut-off [S3].
- **What is contested**: Macroprolactin (prolactin–IgG complexes, biologically inactive) cross-reacts in immunoassays. Fahie-Wilson et al. 2022: "All prolactin assays currently available are affected with 5-25% of results" indicating hyperprolactinaemia falsely elevated due to macroprolactinaemia [S4]. Suliman et al. 2003 reviewed women at a tertiary centre with prolactin >1000 mIU/L and "identified 21 patients in whom hyperprolactinemia was accounted for entirely by the presence of macroprolactin"; among them "pituitary imaging was performed in 93%" and dopamine-agonist treatment prescribed in 87% [S5]. Since women have both a higher upper limit and most prolactin requests, this confound falls mainly on women.
- **What a reader with a mildly raised value is told**: Endocrine Society 2011, Recommendation 1.1: "To establish the diagnosis of hyperprolactinemia, we recommend a single measurement of serum prolactin; a level above the upper limit of normal confirms the diagnosis as long as the serum sample was obtained without excessive venipuncture stress"; Recommendation 1.2: "In patients with asymptomatic hyperprolactinemia, we suggest assessing for macroprolactin" [S3].
- **Confidence**: high for lab ranges and guideline wording; medium for the macroprolactin prevalence figure (abstract-level).

### Vitamin B12 and folate (brief)

- **Units**: B12 pg/mL (= ng/L) conventional, pmol/L SI; 200 ng/L = 148 pmol/L (factor 0.738) [S3]. Folate ng/mL (= µg/L) conventional, nmol/L SI; 1 ng/mL = 2.266 nmol/L (standard factor; not separately verified).
- **Typical lab reference range**: LabCorp vitamin B12 (test 001503, ECLIA): "232−1245 pg/mL", no sex-specific values [S1]. LabCorp folate, serum (test 002014, ECLIA): ">3.0 ng/mL", no sex-specific values; the page notes a serum folate below 3 ng/mL is considered clinical deficiency by the WHO [S2].
- **Where that range came from**: Manufacturer intervals (Roche ECLIA). The historical B12 decision point of about 200 ng/L is older than current assays: Harrington et al. 2025 say the clinical decision point is "commonly set in the region of 200 ng/L (148 pmol/L)" and that this figure derives partly from older radioassay and microbiological methods, not current clinical assays [S3].
- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: None found. (B12 falls in pregnancy and the guidelines treat pregnancy as an exception for interpretation, per Harrington 2025 [S3]; no numeric pregnancy threshold was verified.)
- **What is contested**: The lower limit. BSH 2014 (Devalia et al.): no gold-standard test exists, serum cobalamin is first-line, methylmalonic acid the usual second-line test, and "cut-off values for deficiency cannot be standardised across methods", so local reference ranges are recommended [S4]. NICE NG239 (2024) could not be opened (403), but two 2025–2026 peer-reviewed papers describe it: "NICE NG239 suggests an indeterminate range of 180 to 350 ng/L (133 to 258 pmol/L)" and "MMA measurement should be considered in people who have symptoms or signs of B12 deficiency" [S3]; and a UK audit found "Only 16.6 % of laboratories used the NG239 best-practice threshold of 180 ng/L (133 pmol/L)", with reported UK thresholds ranging from 65 to 239 ng/L and the Beckman DxI assay requiring a lower cut-off of about 145 ng/L [S5]. In US units this is the 200-versus-300 pg/mL debate: LabCorp's lower limit (232 pg/mL) sits inside the NICE "indeterminate" band (180–350). "There is no 'gold standard' test for deficiency" [S3].
- **What a reader with a value in the indeterminate band (about 180–350 pg/mL) is told**: per Harrington 2025's account of NG239, MMA "should be considered in people who have symptoms or signs of B12 deficiency"; BSH 2014: treatment should not be delayed "when clinical features strongly suggest deficiency but test results disagree" (paraphrase from abstract) [S3, S4].
- **Confidence**: medium. Lab ranges verified; NICE wording is second-hand (two peer-reviewed descriptions); WHO 2015 folate cut-offs could not be opened (403) and are **unverified** beyond LabCorp's attribution of the 3 ng/mL figure to the WHO.
