# Batch A: iron and blood count

Research notes for "Normal for whom?" Compiled 29 October 2026. Every number below was read from a source that was opened during this session (lab catalogue page, guideline text, journal abstract or full text). Where a claim could not be read from a primary source it is marked **unverified**. Verbatim quotations are in quotation marks and attributed to the numbered source for that analyte.

Access notes that affect what is and is not verified:

- Mayo Clinic Laboratories, the WHO ferritin guideline (NCBI Bookshelf copy), and the BMJ were read through the desktop browser pane because their servers refuse automated fetches.
- The WHO 2024 haemoglobin guideline was read from the WHO PDF (a public mirror copy of the WHO file, ISBN 978-92-4-008854-2, suggested citation and licence intact).
- NICE Clinical Knowledge Summaries are geo-restricted to UK addresses; the widely repeated CKS wording "a serum ferritin level of less than 30 micrograms/L confirms iron deficiency" is therefore **unverified** here.

### Ferritin

- **Units**: ng/mL (conventional, US) and µg/L (SI, UK/Australia). Conversion factor 1 (1 ng/mL = 1 µg/L). No arithmetic needed.

- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - Mayo Clinic Laboratories, test FERR1 "Ferritin, Serum", method "Electrochemiluminescence Immunoassay" [S1]. Reference values, quoted exactly: "Males: ... > or =18 years: 31-409 mcg/L"; "Females: ... 18-50 years: 6-175 mcg/L"; "> or =51 years: 11-328 mcg/L". Sex-specific: yes, and age-split for women at 50/51.
  - Labcorp, test 004598 "Ferritin", method "Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA)" [S2]. The page carries two tables. The short table gives "Adult" male 30–400 ng/mL and female 15–150 ng/mL; the age-banded table gives female 20–40 y 30–150, rising with age to >80 y 30–397, and male 20 y and over 30–400. The page also states: "Labcorp has adopted a threshold of 30 ng/mL in adult and adolescent patients, to identify patients with iron deficiency." Sex-specific: yes.
  - ARUP Laboratories, test 0070065 "Ferritin", "Quantitative Chemiluminescent Immunoassay", reference interval "Effective February 22, 2022" [S3]. The adult rows were garbled in extraction and only the 60-years-and-older row is unambiguous: male 31–409, female 11–328 ng/mL (identical to Mayo's upper age band). Sex-specific: yes.

- **Where that range came from**: Mayo's catalogue cites no reference-interval study for ferritin; its "Clinical Reference" list is three textbook chapters (Henry's Clinical Diagnosis and Management by Laboratory Methods, 21st ed., 2007; Tietz Textbook of Clinical Chemistry, 2012 and 6th ed., 2018) [S1]. Mayo's own clinical text says: "In most normal adults, serum ferritin concentrations vary with age and sex ... There is no sex difference until the onset of puberty, at which time ferritin concentrations rise, particularly in male patients. There is a significant positive correlation between age and serum ferritin concentrations in female patients but not in male patients" [S1]. Labcorp and ARUP likewise give no population description. The identity of the Mayo and ARUP 60+ rows (31–409 / 11–328) suggests a common upstream source, most likely the assay manufacturer's expected-value table, but that could not be opened and is **unverified**. The important point for the site is that the female lower limit (6 µg/L at Mayo, 15 at Labcorp) is a statistical lower bound of an apparently healthy population, not a threshold at which iron stores are known to be adequate; this is precisely the objection raised by Rushton et al. [S9] and by the Mei et al. physiological analysis [S5].

- **Official sex-specific thresholds**:
  - WHO 2020, Table 1 [S4]: iron deficiency in apparently healthy individuals is "<12" µg/L for 0–59 months and "<15" µg/L for everyone aged 5 years and over, including "Adults (20–59 years)" and "Older persons (60+ years)"; pregnant women "<15 (first trimester)". With infection or inflammation: "<30" (children under 5) and "<70" (5 years and over). The deficiency threshold is therefore not sex-specific. Risk of iron overload is sex-specific: ">150 females", ">200 males" in apparently healthy people, ">500" in non-healthy adults. Recommendation 1.3 states this as: "A ferritin concentration exceeding 150 μg/L in menstruating women and 200 μg/L in men and non-menstruating women who are otherwise healthy may indicate a risk of iron overload (conditional recommendation, based on previous WHO recommendation)."
  - WHO 2020 rationale on sex: "The available studies were not sufficient to justify a change in current ferritin cut-off values to define iron deficiency and risk of iron overload by sex or age groups." [S4]
  - CDC 1998 (US): "A value of ≤15 µg/L confirms iron deficiency in anemic women"; the report notes a <12 µg/L cutoff gives specificity 100% but sensitivity 61% [S8].
  - EASL 2022 haemochromatosis guideline (upper end, sex-specific): HFE genotyping in "females with transferrin saturation >45% and serum ferritin >200 lg/L and males with transferrin saturation >50% and ferritin >300 lg/L" (LoE 2, strong recommendation) [S10].

- **What is contested**:
  1. The 15 µg/L threshold is expert opinion, not a measured physiological boundary. WHO's own 2020 evidence summary: in the Cochrane diagnostic-accuracy review, "the mean ferritin concentration in healthy individuals was 15.1 μg/L (9 studies, 390 participants) when bone marrow iron content was undetectable, and 70.4 μg/L (3 studies, 151 participants) when bone marrow iron was detectable"; for non-pregnant women specifically only 2 studies, means "12.5 ± 11.2 μg/L and 13.21 ± 22.41 μg/L", and the GRADE certainty "is very low in both cases, owing to risk of bias, serious indirectness, and imprecision" [S4]. Recommendation 1.1 (use ferritin to diagnose iron deficiency) is graded "strong recommendation, low certainty of evidence" [S4].
  2. Mei et al. 2021, Lancet Haematology (DOI 10.1016/S2352-3026(21)00168-X) [S5]: NHANES 2003–06, 2007–10 and 2015–18; 7,498 non-pregnant women aged 15–49 (of 10,462 screened) and 2,569 children aged 12–59 months. Using two independent markers of iron-deficient erythropoiesis, the ferritin concentration at which haemoglobin begins to fall was 25.2 µg/L (95% CI 24.2–26.2) in women and the soluble transferrin receptor inflection was 24.0 µg/L (23.3–24.6); in children 19.9 (18.8–22.6) and 20.0 (19.4–20.9). The paper states the WHO thresholds "were developed on the basis of expert opinion" and that WHO judged the certainty for the <12 and <15 cutoffs "low to very low". The Columbia University press release for the study reports prevalence of iron deficiency in US premenopausal women of about 17% under the WHO threshold versus about 30% under the physiological threshold, and quotes Brittenham: "the WHO thresholds seem to identify a more advanced stage of iron deficiency" and "it would be premature to adopt these thresholds now in clinical practice" [S6].
  3. British Society of Gastroenterology 2021 (Snook et al., Gut, DOI 10.1136/gutjnl-2021-325210) [S7]: "An SF level of <15 µg/L is indicative of absent iron stores"; "SF levels of less than 30 µg/L are generally indicative of low body iron stores"; the lower limit of normal in most laboratories is 15–30 µg/L; a cut-off of 15 has specificity 0.99 and "A cut-off of 45 µg/L provides a respectable specificity of 0.92". Recommendation 4: "Serum ferritin is the single most useful marker of IDA" (medium evidence, strong).
  4. American Society of Hematology 2026 guideline for diagnosis of iron deficiency (Powers et al., Blood Advances, DOI 10.1182/bloodadvances.2025015950, published 16 September 2026) [S11]. Verified from the abstract: "Existing guidance on the diagnostic criteria for iron deficiency is limited and not evidence based"; "The panel agreed on 5 recommendations"; the key recommendations are "serum ferritin thresholds for specific populations with the goal to prioritize the diagnosis of iron deficiency, irrespective of the presence of anemia". The threshold wording as reported by trade press (secondary; full text not opened) [S12, S13]: adults, menstruating and pregnant individuals "30 ng/mL or lower ... instead of 15 ng/mL or lower"; children 9 months to 4 years "20 ng/mL or lower ... instead of 12 ng/mL or lower"; "A threshold of 50 ng/mL or lower may be appropriate" with symptoms, heavy or abnormal uterine bleeding, or anaemia in pregnancy; with inflammation "ferritin below 100 ng/mL or TSAT below 20%"; all recommendations conditional on low or very-low certainty evidence except a strong recommendation against using 15 ng/mL in pregnancy.
  5. Labcorp has already moved its clinical decision limit: "Labcorp has adopted a threshold of 30 ng/mL in adult and adolescent patients, to identify patients with iron deficiency", while still printing a female reference interval that starts at 15 [S2].
  6. The sex-split reference range itself: Rushton et al., BMJ 2001 (DOI 10.1136/bmj.322.7298.1355) [S9] argued that female reference limits for haemoglobin and ferritin are drawn from populations containing many iron-deficient women. Opening text: "In prepubertal humans no major differences can be found between the sexes in red blood cell count or haemoglobin and serum ferritin concentrations. Only after the onset of menstruation does a difference emerge." The replies [S9a] accepted that "marked sex differences in ferritin distributions in a 'normal' population occur because the iron requirements of menstruating women are higher and intakes lower", and argued that for ferritin "the lower limit of the reference range ... should perhaps instead be based on biological definitions of iron deficiency" and that "most would agree that sex specific cut-off points are inappropriate for ferritin when defining iron deficiency" (Morison and Ferguson). In other words both sides of the 2001 exchange agree the female ferritin reference range describes a population, not health.
  - Contested band for a non-pregnant adult woman: roughly 15–30 µg/L (WHO deficiency threshold up to BSG/Labcorp/ASH working threshold), extending to 45–50 µg/L in the BSG sensitivity analysis and the ASH higher-risk remark.

- **What a reader with a value inside the contested band is told by the guidelines**:
  - WHO 2020 [S4]: "Ferritin concentration is a good marker of iron stores and should be used to diagnose iron deficiency in otherwise apparently healthy individuals (strong recommendation, low certainty of evidence)"; below 15 µg/L is the cut-off; "In individuals with infection or inflammation, a ferritin concentration below 30 μg/L in children and 70 μg/L in adults may be used to indicate iron deficiency (conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence)."
  - BSG 2021 [S7]: "SF levels of less than 30 µg/L are generally indicative of low body iron stores"; "The cancer risk in iron deficiency without anaemia is low" (Statement 9, high evidence, strong); "There is insufficient evidence to support invasive investigation in non-anaemic iron deficiency" unless other indications are present (Statement 22, low evidence, weak); periodic blood-count monitoring is suggested, and the text says iron replacement may be offered if symptomatic.
  - Mayo interpretation text [S1]: "Latent iron deficiency occurs when serum ferritin is low without low hemoglobin."
  - ASH 2026 (secondary wording) [S12]: iron deficiency should be diagnosed at ferritin ≤30 ng/mL "irrespective of the presence of anemia", and a detailed menstrual history should be taken to identify abnormal uterine bleeding.
  - NICE CKS: **unverified** (UK-only access).

- **Confidence**: high. The lab ranges, the WHO table and rationale, the Mei thresholds, the BSG statements and the EASL thresholds were all read from primary text; only the ASH recommendation wording and the NICE CKS wording are second-hand.

### Haemoglobin

- **Units**: g/dL (US) and g/L (SI, UK/WHO); g/L = g/dL × 10. Some European labs report mmol/L; the conventional factor is mmol/L = g/dL × 0.6206 (standard conversion, not taken from a cited source here).

- **Typical lab reference range**:
  - Mayo Clinic Laboratories, test CBC "Complete Blood Cell Count (CBC) with Differential, Blood" [H1]. Quoted exactly: Hemoglobin "Males: ... > or =18 years: 13.2-16.6 g/dL"; "Females: ... > or =18 years: 11.6-15.0 g/dL". Sex-specific: yes, from age 11 onward (female 11–17 y 11.9–14.8; male 11–14 y 12.4–15.7, 15–17 y 13.3–16.9).
  - Labcorp, test 005009 CBC with differential: the catalogue page gives units only ("Automated cell counter with mixed technologies") [H2]; the published sample report (a 15-year-old male) prints Hemoglobin reference interval 12.6–17.7 g/dL [H2a]. Adult female interval not obtainable from the public page.

- **Where that range came from**: Mayo's CBC page cites, first, Adeli K, Raizman J, Chen Y, et al., Clin Chem 2015;61(8):1075–1086, and second, CLSI EP28-A3c (the 2008 standard for defining reference intervals) [H1]. Adeli et al. (DOI 10.1373/clinchem.2015.240531) is a CALIPER / Canadian Health Measures Survey collaboration: "The CHMS collected data and blood samples from 11 999 respondents aged 3-79 years"; haematology was measured on "Beckman Coulter HmX or Siemens Sysmex CA-500 Series analyzers"; after exclusion criteria and outlier removal, age- and sex-partitioned intervals were calculated, and the abstract reports that "females had lower hemoglobin and hematocrit starting at puberty" [H3]. The abstract does not describe iron-status exclusions, so whether iron-deficient women were removed before the female interval was set is **unverified** from the abstract. The Mayo female lower limit (11.6 g/dL) is therefore a population 2.5th centile from a Canadian general-population survey, and it sits below the WHO anaemia cutoff of 12.0 g/dL.

- **Official sex-specific thresholds**:
  - WHO 2024 guideline, Table 2 (haemoglobin cutoffs to define anaemia, g/L, "Based on 5th percentile") [H4]: "Adults, 15–65 years, nonpregnant women <120"; "Adults, 15–65 years, men <130"; "Children, 12–14 years, nonpregnant girls <120"; "Children, 12–14 years, boys <120"; pregnancy first trimester <110, second <105, third <110; children 6–23 months <105, 24–59 months <110, 5–11 years <115. Severity (Table 3): non-pregnant women mild 110–119, moderate 80–109, severe <80; men mild 110–129, moderate 80–109, severe <80.
  - CDC 1998 (US), Table 6 [H8]: anaemia is Hb below 12.0 g/dL for "Nonpregnant and lactating women ≥18 yr" and below 13.5 g/dL for "Men ≥18 yr"; cutoffs are "the 5th percentile of Hb or Hct in a healthy reference population from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III)" with "Persons with a high likelihood of iron deficiency ... excluded"; smokers' cutoffs adjusted upward by 0.3 g/dL.
  - BSG 2021 adopts the WHO limits: anaemia is Hb "below 130 g/L in men over 15 years of age", below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women over 15, and below 110 g/L in the second and third trimester; Recommendation 3 says anaemia should be defined by the local laboratory's lower limit of normal, which should align with WHO [H9].

- **What is contested**:
  1. Origin. The WHO 2024 guideline itself documents that the adult cutoffs "were first proposed in 1968 after technical meetings of clinical and public health experts working with data from five studies of populations in Europe and North America with predominantly European ancestry", that they "were based on four published references and one set of unpublished observations", and that "These studies were done in an era when laboratory and epidemiological methods were less developed than today" [H4]. WHO first proposed cutoffs in 1959 (14 g/dL men, 12 g/dL women) and the 1968 report (Technical Report Series 405) moved men to 13 and kept women at 12 [H4, H7]. The 1968 report was not opened directly; its values are taken from the 2024 guideline's history section and Table 1.
  2. Beutler and Waalen, Blood 2006 (DOI 10.1182/blood-2005-07-3046) [H5]: using the Scripps-Kaiser database (San Diego, 1998–2002) with NHANES-III as "confirmatory", excluding transferrin saturation <16% or ferritin <10 µg/L, pregnancy, alcohol excess, diabetes, renal failure and raised inflammatory markers, and taking the 5th percentile as mean − 1.65 SD, they proposed lower limits of 12.2 g/dL for white women aged 20–49 (n = 2,966 Scripps-Kaiser; 1,045 NHANES-III) and 13.7 g/dL for white men 20–59 (n = 6,709; 1,456), with 11.5 g/dL for Black women and 12.9 g/dL for Black men. They wrote that the WHO numbers were "casually presented in that document", "based on very few data using methods that were inadequate", and given "in whole numbers without decimal points", which "clearly suggests that the authors never intended for these values to become a hallowed international standard". Note the direction: in an iron-replete population the female limit rises only slightly (12.0 to 12.2) while the male limit rises more (13.0 to 13.7), so the sex gap persists after iron deficiency is excluded.
  3. Braat et al., Lancet Haematology 2024 (DOI 10.1016/S2352-3026(24)00030-9), the WHO-commissioned analysis behind the 2024 table [H6]: pooled fifth centiles from eight data sources (18 datasets, 1998–2020) were 119.7 g/L (90% CI 119.1–120.3) in 3,640 non-pregnant females aged 18–65 and 134.9 g/L (134.2–135.6) in 2,377 males; female adolescents 12–17 y 122.2 g/L, male adolescents 128.2 g/L. "Thresholds are similar between sexes until adolescence, after which males have higher thresholds than females." The reference population was defined by WHO as people "without reported underlying medical conditions; recent clinical illness; biochemical evidence of iron deficiency or inflammation, other nutrient deficiencies, or renal/hepatic impairment (where available)" [H4]. The WHO Guideline Development Group then noted: "Data in adult men may suggest a higher haemoglobin cutoff (i.e. 135 g/L) but there was uncertainty in the evidence. The GDG decided to retain the cutoff of 130 g/L for men after considering concerns about: iron therapy implications for males; the limited evidence; and the potential unintended consequences of changing the cutoff" [H4]. So the current women's cutoff (120) matches the iron-replete fifth centile (119.7), whereas the men's cutoff is held about 5 g/L below the data.
  4. The "is the gap just iron deficiency" debate. Rushton et al., BMJ 2001 [H10] argued that women's lower reference limits reflect reference populations containing many iron-deficient women ("Women worldwide are at risk of being in a negative iron balance, and by current criteria if their haemoglobin concentration is less than 115 g/l they are deemed to be anaemic, whereas in men the cut-off point is 130 g/l"). Morison and Ferguson replied with iron-replete data: 612 men and 1,327 women aged 20–45 with ferritin 80–100 µg/L had 10th/50th centiles of 139/152 g/L (men) versus 123/135 g/L (women); a New Zealand nutrition survey subset with ferritin 50–100 µg/L and normal CRP gave 140/150 (n = 110 men) versus 127/138 g/L (n = 252 women); "depending on the choice of the lower limit of haemoglobin for men (usually 130-135 g/l), 25-50% of women would be incorrectly classified as anaemic if a male reference range was used" [H10a]. Heath, Fairweather-Tait and Worwood: "We believe that there is no evidence to support reclassification of haemoglobin and serum concentrations of ferritin in women to normal values for men" [H10a]. A further reply by Waalen, Felitti and Beutler (BMJ 2002;325:137) rejecting the iron-deficiency explanation was identified in search results but not opened (**unverified**).
  5. Contested band for a non-pregnant adult woman: 11.6–12.0 g/dL (116–120 g/L), i.e. values the Mayo reference interval reports as normal but WHO, CDC and BSG classify as mild anaemia; plus the structural question of whether a 12.0 g/dL threshold under-diagnoses women relative to the 13.0 g/dL threshold men receive.

- **What a reader with a value inside the contested band is told by the guidelines**:
  - WHO 2024 [H4]: non-pregnant women 15–65 years with 110–119 g/L have "Mild anaemia" (Table 3); the guideline adds that "The management for individuals with mild, moderate, or severe anaemia could vary depending on the clinical scenario and the underlying cause(s) of the anaemia."
  - BSG 2021 [H9]: "We recommend that iron deficiency should be confirmed by iron studies prior to investigation. Serum ferritin is the single most useful marker of IDA" (Recommendation 4); a Hb rise of at least 10 g/L within 2 weeks of oral iron "is highly suggestive of absolute iron deficiency" (Recommendation 5); for non-anaemic iron deficiency the cancer risk "is low" and invasive investigation is not supported.
  - CDC 1998 [H8]: ferritin ≤15 µg/L "confirms iron deficiency in anemic women".
  - Mayo CBC interpretation [H1]: "Results outside of normal value ranges may reflect a primary disorder of the cell-producing organs or an underlying disease. Results should be interpreted in conjunction with the patient's clinical picture and appropriate additional testing performed."

- **Confidence**: high. WHO 2024 tables and history, Braat 2024, Beutler and Waalen 2006, the BMJ exchange, CDC 1998 and the Mayo page were all read from primary text. The 1968 WHO report and the Waalen 2002 letter were not opened.

### Haematocrit

- **Units**: % (conventional) and L/L (SI); L/L = % ÷ 100.

- **Typical lab reference range**: Mayo Clinic Laboratories CBC [H1]: "Hematocrit ... Males: ... > or =18 years: 38.3-48.6%"; "Females: ... > or =18 years: 35.5-44.9%". Sex-specific: yes, diverging from age 12 (male 12–15 y 38–47%; female 8–17 y 35–43%). Labcorp's sample report (15-year-old male) prints 37.5–51.0% [H2a].

- **Where that range came from**: as for haemoglobin, Mayo cites Adeli et al. 2015 (Canadian Health Measures Survey, 11,999 respondents aged 3–79, Beckman Coulter HmX / Sysmex analysers) and CLSI EP28-A3c [H1, H3]. Adeli et al. report that "females had lower hemoglobin and hematocrit starting at puberty" [H3].

- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: CDC 1998, Table 6 [H8]: anaemia is haematocrit below 35.7% for "Nonpregnant and lactating women ≥18 yr" and below 39.9% for "Men ≥18 yr" (5th percentile of NHANES III after excluding people likely to be iron deficient; smokers' cutoffs raised by 1.0 percentage point). WHO's 2024 anaemia guideline is written in haemoglobin only; its cutoff table contains no haematocrit column [H4]. No UK or Australian official haematocrit cutoff was opened.

- **What is contested**: haematocrit tracks haemoglobin (it is the packed red-cell fraction, and on automated analysers is calculated from red-cell count and MCV), so the dispute is the same one described under haemoglobin: the female limit derives from a general-population centile [H3], while the iron-replete fifth-centile analyses (Beutler and Waalen 2006 [H5]; Braat 2024 [H6]) and the BMJ 2001 exchange [H10, H10a] were argued in haemoglobin terms. No haematocrit-specific threshold study was found. Contested band for an adult woman: roughly 35.5–35.7% is the only direct overlap between the Mayo lower limit and the CDC anaemia cutoff; more usefully, the Mayo interval's lower limit (35.5%) is just below the CDC definition of anaemia (35.7%), so a reader at 35.6% is "normal" at Mayo and anaemic by CDC.

- **What a reader with a value inside the contested band is told**: CDC 1998 treats a haematocrit below 35.7% in a non-pregnant woman as anaemia, and says ferritin ≤15 µg/L then "confirms iron deficiency in anemic women" [H8]. Mayo's CBC interpretation text applies ("Results should be interpreted in conjunction with the patient's clinical picture and appropriate additional testing performed") [H1]. BSG 2021 frames investigation around haemoglobin and ferritin rather than haematocrit [H9].

- **Confidence**: medium. The lab range and CDC cutoffs are primary; there is no haematocrit-specific contested-threshold literature in this batch, so the "contested" entry is inherited from haemoglobin.

- **Sources**: H1, H2a, H3, H4, H5, H6, H8, H9, H10 as listed under Haemoglobin.

### Transferrin saturation (and serum iron)

- **Units**: transferrin saturation is a percentage (iron ÷ total iron-binding capacity × 100), identical in conventional and SI reporting. Serum iron: µg/dL (conventional) and µmol/L (SI); µmol/L = µg/dL × 0.179 (standard conversion).

- **Typical lab reference range**: Mayo Clinic Laboratories, test SFEC "Iron and Total Iron-Binding Capacity, Serum" [T1]. Quoted exactly: "IRON Males: 50-150 mcg/dL Females: 35-145 mcg/dL"; "TOTAL IRON-BINDING CAPACITY 250-400 mcg/dL"; "PERCENT SATURATION 14-50%". Sex-specific: serum iron yes; saturation no. Labcorp's iron and TIBC page (test 001321, "Colorimetric") gives units only [T2]; its sample report (32-year-old male) prints Iron 38–169 ug/dL, TIBC 250–450, Iron Saturation 15–55% [T2a].

- **Where that range came from**: Mayo's page cites textbook and guideline sources (Tietz Textbook of Laboratory Medicine, 7th ed., 2023; the ACG 2019 hereditary haemochromatosis guideline; Lopez et al., Lancet 2016) and no reference-interval study [T1]. Mayo warns that "The intraindividual day-to-day variation of iron and iron saturation is approximately 25% to 30%" and that the test "should not be used as the primary test for iron deficiency" because "serum ferritin is a much more sensitive and reliable test" [T1]. The provenance of the 14–50% interval is therefore **unverified** beyond these textbook citations.

- **Official sex-specific thresholds**:
  - Lower end (iron deficiency), not sex-specific: CDC 1998: "A value below 16% in adults is often used to confirm iron deficiency" [T4]; Mayo: "Percent saturation less than 16% is generally used to screen for iron deficiency, but a threshold of 20% is used in the presence of inflammation" [T1]; BSG 2021: "A transferrin saturation of <20% is indicative of iron restriction", and in chronic kidney disease absolute iron deficiency is TSAT ≤20% with ferritin ≤100 µg/L (predialysis) [T5]; ASH 2026 (secondary wording): with inflammation, iron deficiency "may be considered with ferritin below 100 ng/mL or TSAT below 20%" [T6].
  - Upper end (iron overload), sex-specific: EASL 2022: "A transferrin saturation >50% in men and >45% in women has been defined as elevated"; HFE genotyping is recommended for "females with transferrin saturation >45% and serum ferritin >200 lg/L and males with transferrin saturation >50% and ferritin >300 lg/L" (LoE 2, strong recommendation), and the guideline notes "There is no universally accepted threshold for ferritin or transferrin saturation indicating iron overload, but one seminal population screening study identified" these values [T3]. Mayo: "Percent saturation often exceeds 45% in hereditary hemochromatosis" [T1]. The British Society for Haematology 2018 raised-ferritin guideline (Cullis et al., DOI 10.1111/bjh.15166) was not accessible (publisher refused the fetch); its thresholds are **unverified** here.

- **What is contested**: the iron-deficiency cutoff is a convention (16% versus 20%) rather than a sex-specific question, and the reference interval's lower limit (14% at Mayo) sits below both conventional deficiency thresholds, so a woman at 15% is inside the Mayo reference interval while below the CDC deficiency cutoff [T1, T4]. The sex-specific element is at the top: women are flagged for haemochromatosis work-up at a lower saturation (>45%) than men (>50%) [T3]. The BSG guideline lists serum ferritin, not saturation, as "the single most useful marker" and reserves TSAT for suspected false-normal ferritin [T5]. Contested band for a woman: 14–20% (within the Mayo interval but at or below the CDC/BSG deficiency thresholds).

- **What a reader with a value inside the contested band is told**: Mayo: "Percent saturation less than 16% is generally used to screen for iron deficiency, but a threshold of 20% is used in the presence of inflammation" and the test "may be helpful in conjunction with ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor, especially in patients with inflammation" [T1]. BSG: "Transferrin saturation can help if a false-normal ferritin is suspected" (Recommendation 4) [T5]. CDC: below 16% "is often used to confirm iron deficiency" [T4].

- **Confidence**: medium. Thresholds were read from primary text, but the reference-interval provenance is textbook-level and the BSH 2018 document could not be opened.

### Mean corpuscular volume (MCV)

- **Units**: femtolitres (fL) in both conventional and SI reporting; factor 1.

- **Typical lab reference range**: Mayo Clinic Laboratories CBC [M1]: "Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) ... Males: ... > or =18 years: 78.2-97.9 fL"; "Females: ... > or =18 years: 78.2-97.9 fL". Sex-specific: no; Mayo prints the same interval for adult men and women (and identical paediatric bands from age 3). Labcorp's sample report (15-year-old male) prints 79–97 fL [M2a].

- **Where that range came from**: Mayo cites Adeli et al. 2015 (Canadian Health Measures Survey, 11,999 respondents aged 3–79, Beckman Coulter HmX / Sysmex) and CLSI EP28-A3c [M1, M3]. Mayo's clinical text: "Anemias are characterized as microcytic (MCV <80), macrocytic (MCV >100), or normocytic" [M1].

- **Official sex-specific thresholds**: none found. CDC 1998 defines a low MCV as a value below "the 5th percentile for age in NHANES III" without printing a sex-specific number, and says a low MCV with RDW above 14.0% points to iron-deficiency anaemia rather than thalassaemia minor [M4]. BSG 2021 uses MCV as a risk marker rather than a threshold: "Age, sex, Hb concentration and mean cell volume are all independent predictors of risk of GI cancer in IDA, and need to be considered as part of a holistic risk assessment" (Statement 9, high evidence, strong) [M5].

- **What is contested**: nothing sex-specific was found. The interval is one of the few in this batch that is not partitioned by sex, and the two published working cutoffs (microcytic <80 fL at Mayo; <78.2 fL as the Mayo lower reference limit) differ by about 2 fL [M1]. No study arguing that the female MCV interval is set on the wrong population was identified. Contested band: none established (a 78.2–80 fL grey zone exists between Mayo's reference limit and its microcytosis definition, but this is a definitional gap, not a sex controversy).

- **What a reader with a value inside the grey zone is told**: Mayo: "Anemias are characterized as microcytic (MCV <80), macrocytic (MCV >100), or normocytic" and "iron deficiency anemia is characterized by a high RDW, while thalassemia is characterized by a low RDW" [M1]. CDC: low MCV plus RDW above 14.0% "points to iron-deficiency anemia rather than thalassemia minor" [M4]. BSG: MCV contributes to cancer-risk assessment in iron-deficiency anaemia but does not itself trigger investigation [M5].

- **Confidence**: medium. The lab interval and its cited provenance are primary; the absence of a sex-specific controversy is a negative finding, which cannot be fully proven.
